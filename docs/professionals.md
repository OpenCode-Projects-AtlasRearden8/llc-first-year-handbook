# When to call a professional

Somewhere between "I'm fine winging it" and "I hired a firm at week two out of anxiety" lives a small, knowable art: **recognizing the moment a real professional earns their fee, then arriving with clean pages.** This chapter is that art.

Two truths to hold before the lists:

1. **Almost every year-one situation is fixable afterward.** Entities get corrected, ledgers get reconciled, estimates get recalculated. What *isn't* cheap to fix is the delay — penalties compound and stakes mature. So the skill isn't panic; it's *early recognition*.
2. **A professional charges by the hour only until the relationship exists.** After one good engagement, "quick question" email threads are cheap, and the return is years of calm. Hiring one isn't defeat; it's what the [money dates](money-dates.md) are largely *holding money aside* to make possible.

## The triggers (the heart of this chapter)

When any of these happens — no matter how small that quarter felt — open your calendar, not just a browser tab:

1. **A government letter arrives.** IRS, Indiana DOR, Indiana SOS, anything. Read it aloud once, then: the due date goes on the calendar the same day you open it. Most letters are mundane. Every one deserves a *dated response*, which is exactly what professionals are best at.
2. **Profit accelerates past the pot.** The [25–30% set-aside](taxes.md) is a starter rule of thumb. When quarterly profit begins generating numbers you don't have an intuition for — that is the moment for a **projection sit-down**, where a tax professional runs an actual estimate method instead of a round number.
3. **You're about to make an entity-structure decision.** S-corp election talk, adding a second owner, hiring your first contractor or employee, a second company. Each of these has real, permanent consequences; each costs one consult and saves multiples of it.
4. **A contract with teeth:** publishing agreement with signature, exclusive distribution deal, an app-store or platform contract larger than lunch, an NDA with a company bigger than your grocery store. These shift risk *onto you*; that's the lawyer half of the professional ledger.
5. **Hiring humans**, whether contractor (W-9 / payment-reporting land) or employee (withholding, insurance, state registrations). The launch rules from this handbook mostly *sunset here* — hiring is a whole new map, and the professional hands you the right one.
6. **Multi-state reality arrives:** selling through platforms that create economic presence elsewhere, a client in another state doing something formal, considering a move. Nexus (the "you're now ours too" rule) is a pro question, full stop.
7. **Insurance exposure shifts:** new product routed through clinics? New physical equipment in the public's path? A venue demanding named coverage? Quotes and endorsements change when operations change.
8. **A claim actually happens** — some bodily damage, a customer dispute escalating, a cease-and-desist about published text. Insurance and lawyer, *before responding substantively to the other side.* The most expensive response in any of these stories is the free one sent in a frustrated hour.
9. **Status weirdness at the SOS** — anything other than "active/good standing", per [the annual-report ritual](annual-report.md). Filing status errors snowball into tax-status errors and worse.
10. **The undefinable.** That sentence — "I feel uneasy about this and I can't articulate why" — is a diagnostic in itself. Undefinable unease + an hour of a professional's time is cheaper than its alternatives.

None of these says your bookkeeping is broken or you did something wrong. They say: **the situation's complexity has outgrown the starter manual** — which means year one did its job and taught you what to ask.

## The roles, sorted so you hire the right one

This is the no-acronyms version:

| You need… | The person | What they don't do |
|---|---|---|
| Books kept, reconciled, quarterly tuneups | **Bookkeeper** | Legal or tax positions |
| Returns prepared, estimated payments calculated, letters answered | **Tax preparer** | Business/structure decisions beyond the return |
| Return prep *plus* planning, audits representation, deeper tax judgment | **CPA** | Usually not: legal drafting |
| The same tax-side depth (federally licensed; often more available year-round) | **Enrolled agent (EA)** | Business/structure legal decisions |
| Contracts, disputes, structural/asset questions, letters with lawyers on them | **Attorney** | Tax numbers (beyond flagging when to loop one in) |
| Quotes, COIs, coverage gaps, endorsements | **Independent insurance agent** | Judge "which policy is best" — they present, you decide |
| Mail and a legal address for the company | **Registered agent service** | Anything judgment-related ([see the ritual](annual-report.md)) |

For a year-one solo content-and-tools LLC, the usual year-one hires are: **a tax professional (preparer, EA, or CPA) once in launch season** and **nothing else until a trigger above fires**. Everything else in your corner is either free (the agent can be you), bundled (insurance), or a tool (the ledger).

## Arriving prepared: the folder that makes you cheap to help

Professionals bill in time, and the difference between a $300 engagement and a $900 engagement is often **fourteen minutes of your prep**. Bring:

- [ ] **The LLC papers:** Articles, EIN letter, any assumed-name filing ([bank chapter](bank-accounts.md), where these live)
- [ ] **The ledger:** your full year, plus the [cashflow tax-season export](../cashflow-simple-ledger) — their eyes light up when this is a CSV instead of a shoebox
- [ ] **Last year's personal return** (they need the whole-you picture: W-2s, other income, all of it)
- [ ] **The insurance summaries** (limits, deductibles — one page from the [insurance chapter](insurance.md) evening)
- [ ] **Every letter you're unsure about**, in date order
- [ ] **The questions**, written, on one page, ranked. This is the single biggest cost-saver on the list.

## The first-call script

Keep it under two minutes; write it before dialing:

> "I run a single-member LLC in Indiana — content publishing and small tools, formed October 2026. I keep a clean ledger and set aside percentages. I have [N] questions: [first question]. Do you think this needs an hour now, or should I wait for [upcoming event]? What would you charge?"

What that little paragraph *buys you*: three minutes of triage, a quote before commitment, and the professional's read on urgency — while signaling "this one keeps a ledger," which quietly changes everyone's price and everyone's stress level.

Interview questions, short form:

1. **Are you licensed / what's your specialty?** (Taxes: CPA or EA. Law: your state's bar. Insurance: state licensing. You're politely auditing.)
2. **Have you handled [your exact shape of situation] before?** Authors, small digital sellers, and one-person LLCs each have their own usual answers.
3. **Do you bill flat, hourly, or per-return?** Get numbers before proceedings, in writing if meaningful.
4. **What's the best way to ask you a small question?** Email with expected turnaround is normal and turns an unknown into a system.

And one thing not to ask: "Can you just take everything from here?" That's a grow-the-company question, not a year-one one, and the answer will be a retainer priced for someone further along.

## The two-question triage, when something's on your mind

Before *any* professional (or even a friend in business), answer in one sentence each:

1. **What has to happen, and by when, if I do nothing at all?** (Names the deadline.)
2. **What decision would change if the answer were "yes"/"no"?** (Names the stakes.)

If you can't answer either yet, that's *your* homework hour — the chapters above can almost always name both for you. If you can answer both and *still* feel unease — that's the professional trigger, full stop.

## A note on AI, since this is an AI-author's handbook

AI is a magnificent **preparation engine** and a dangerous **judgment engine**. It excels at: drafting the first-call script, summarizing a government letter in plain English ("translate, don't act"), generating your question list, turning your mess into a timeline for a pro to review. It must not be the final word on tax positions, legal conclusions, or insurance adequacy — the day matters, and hallucinated tax law is punctual about ruining weekends. The [playbook's honest limits chapter](../small-business-ai-playbook) covers the full boundary; this chapter just adds one line to it: **AI prepares; professionals decide.**

## After the fact

- The professional's conclusions go back into the system you already run: the pot percentage becomes the new set-aside rule; contract clauses become new templates; the letter's answer becomes a line in your records. First-year books and folders are the *input* to every future year of cheaper, calmer advice.
- Keep their fee in the ledger under `Professional Fees` — it's an ordinary LLC cost like software, and future-you will send it flowers.

Honestly? The best day of year one is the first time you email "quick question" and get a calm answer by Thursday. Build for that email.
