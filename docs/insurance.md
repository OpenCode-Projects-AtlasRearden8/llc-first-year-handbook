# Insurance starters

Here is the insurance conversation, minus the fog. The LLC formation protects your **personal** assets from the *company's debts and lawsuits*. Insurance hands you back the *company's* money and health when something hurts the company itself. These are different protections stacked like a helmet and a knee brace, and year one only needs a helmet first.

Same promise as every chapter: this is **orientation to what exists**, never advice about what you need. Quotes come from licensed agents; fits come from your actual risks. My risks, for scale: a nurse-turned-author publishing how-to guides, AI manuals, and small tools. If your LLC is a bakery or a law-grade consultancy, the list of relevant policies would reshuffle. The *method* below holds either way.

## The order people actually buy in

For a one-person LLC built on content and tools, here's the standard progression — buy near the top, go down only when the business earns its way further:

1. **General liability (GL)** — the foundation policy
2. **Business owner's policy (BOP)** — GL plus business property, bundled, usually cheaper than separate
3. **Professional liability (E&O)** — the "my advice/product hurt someone's money or trust" policy
4. **Media liability** — the specific cousin for people who *publish*
5. **Cyber** — for anything that touches customer data or online accounts
6. **Health + disability** — the personal-side policies that a solo founder quietly loses when leaving an employer

That last line item deserves a whole paragraph of honesty from someone who made that exact winter-to-independent transition: nursing paychecks teach one thing very well — **an income stream can stop overnight, and while it was coming, it was paying for insurance you never read.** When you become both the employer and the employee, the employer-half of that deal evaporates. Health coverage (marketplace or a spouse's plan — ask, don't guess) and long-term disability (coverage for *your own hands and brain*, which are literally the LLC's entire productive plant) are unglamorous and enormous. They're "starters" in the sense that you start them early and review them forever.

## General liability, in one paragraph

Covers **bodily injury and property damage claims connected to your business operations** — someone trips at your booth, your gear scratches a client's floor, a courier damages a venue. It defends you and pays judgments up to the limit. What it clearly does **not** cover: *your professional mistakes* — advice, designs, code, or content that cost someone money or health. Which brings us to...

## The policy that actually matters for an author-educator LLC

**Professional liability (errors & omissions / E&O)** exists for exactly my-shaped business: we tell people things. If someone uses your guide, your checklist, or your teaching and then suffers a loss, GL shrugs — E&O is the policy that shows up. The education-only-disclaimer stance (which this handbook and everything at [dylanasdale.github.io](https://dylanasdale.github.io) hold) is good practice and *softens* risk; it is not a shield that legally prevents claims. Disclaimer text reduces the *success rate* of a misunderstanding; an E&O policy pays for the *defense lawyer* even when you're clearly right. Both matter, and only one involves a lawyer you'd have to find mid-crisis.

**Media liability** is the publishing-specific sub-flavor: copyright claims, defamation, privacy-of-persons claims threaded through what you published. Some BOPs and professional policies can tack it on; some insurers sell it separately. It's the policy that speaks directly to "I published a book and someone got very upset about one paragraph."

For the small tool-and-content shop I run, the honest conversation I had: GL quote → BOP quote → E&O quote, in that order, prices compared, one evening. Insurers sell it differently; ask for both bundled and unbundled quotes.

## Ballparks, honestly labeled

Founders exchange these numbers constantly, so here they are with their costume still on: small solo-LLC GL policies are *reported* in the **rough hundreds of dollars a year** range, BOPs similar plus property value, E&O for content creators varying widely by limit and niche. They are reported ranges and nothing more — insurance pricing swings on industry codes, revenue, limits, and state. Get real quotes. The *surprise* is usually that the quotes are smaller than the fear.

## The seven questions to take to a quoting conversation

1. What is my NAICS-equivalent classification, and is it what I *do*? (Book publishing, software publishing, and consulting sit in different insuring buckets — classify correctly.)
2. What limits does the policy carry, and what would a claim *actually* exhaust first?
3. Per-occurrence vs. aggregate — in plain English: how much *one incident* can eat versus *all incidents in the year*. Ask for both numbers and write both down.
4. Does the BOP include **business personal property** (laptop, monitor, desk rig), and at replacement value?
5. Is **media liability** included, excluded, or available as an add-on?
6. What's the deductible, and who handles defense costs before or after it?
7. What would make me un-renewable — a product pivot? Revenue growth? Outside says: ask now, not at renewal month.

Certification of insurance (the **COI** — the PDF that proves coverage) goes into `legal/insurance/` the moment it issues... and events and platforms that require it will ask for it constantly. One PDF, filed once, printed whenever a conference table or an app-store program demands proof.

## What most one-person content/tech LLCs *skip* in year one

Said out loud so you know skipping is also a decision and sometimes the right one:

- **High-limit commercial packages** — for teams and trucks you don't have
- **Workers' compensation** — pays when you have employees; solo in Indiana generally means no requirement, confirm current rules for your situation
- **Commercial auto** — if "business vehicle" means a personal car doing occasional supply runs, ask about the personal-policy endorsement question instead
- **Fidelity/crime bonds** — you'd love to steal from yourself, but you're not covered against you anyway
