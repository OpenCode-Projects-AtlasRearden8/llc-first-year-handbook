# Quarterly money dates

If the weekly records are the heartbeat, the **money date** is the quarterly checkup with a doctor who happens to be you. Ninety minutes, four times a year, on a real calendar with a real reservation and at least one good drink. It is a meeting where you:

- look at the whole business's money life at once,
- make the small decisions that ten-minute weeks never settle,
- and *put the next quarter's plan in writing before anyone can talk you out of it.*

The name "date" is on purpose. You don't grind through your finances; you sit with them. Calendar it like a lunch with someone you like. In my FFXIV brain, it's the weekly/daily duty system: short, scheduled, unlocks a reward, never eligible for procrastination because the *timer* — the calendar — does the remembering. My four dates live inside the last two weeks of each quarter. Yours need two anchors a year and then they self-clone.

> **Monthly vs. quarterly?** The [small-business-ai-playbook](../small-business-ai-playbook) already covers a **60-minute monthly money-date**: the light look at income, outflow, and the six questions. This chapter is its bigger sibling — the business-admin quarter in review. You can run both (I do: the monthly one keeps the ledger tidy, this one runs the company) or start with quarterly only and add monthly when it feels good.

## Booking it, once and forever

- **Dates:** Same *day type* every quarter — mine is the second Saturday of the last month of each quarter, 9:30 a.m., with coffee. Automated quarterly: March, June, September, December.
- **Length:** 90 focused minutes. If ADHD energy is tight, see the gentle version below — it's a real version, not a lesser one.
- **Input:** Ledger open, bank sites open, this page and the [money-date checklist](quarterly-money-date.md) printed or on a second screen.
- **Output:** The filled-in checklist. That half-page of hand-writing *is* the quarter's whole purpose.

## The agenda: seven questions

Work in order; each one takes minutes, not hours.

### 1. Close the books (15 min)

Update the ledger through today. Sweep `Uncategorized` to zero. Confirm bank/ledger agree — both sides of the two-pot system. Total income, total expenses, **net profit** for the quarter. These three numbers are the entire scoreboard; write them on the checklist.

### 2. Feed the tax pot (10 min)

Take this quarter's net profit. Apply the set-aside percentage from the [taxes chapter](taxes.md) — plain round numbers there, not here. Move that money from **pot 1 to pot 2** while the meeting is live, so the decision is *physical*, not notional. Update the pot's running total. (The IRS's own wording on these payments lives at [IRS estimated taxes](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/estimated-taxes) — worth one skim per year.) Realize, with a small internal happy noise, that the taxes are basically scheduled now.

### 3. Read the subscriptions ledger (10 min)

This is the quarterly spot where $14.99 apps come to be judged. Every recurring charge: still earning its keep? Cancel with courage. Downgrade what's viable. This line-item hunt alone can pay for the meeting in coffee money — and when it grows into a full-time discipline, our [subscription-audit handbook](../subscription-audit-handbook) is the bigger manual for it.

### 4. Check the people in your corner (10 min)

- **Insurance**: next renewal date visible? Any policy due? Any *new* kind of risk you've picked up this quarter (a new product type, a contract with required coverage)? → [insurance chapter](insurance.md)
- **State items**: this anniversary month or a filing year? Any registered-agency-letter arrived unopened? → [annual-report ritual](annual-report.md)
- **Professional triggers**: did anything happen this quarter that matches a trigger in [when to call a pro](professionals.md)? If yes, that's on the calendar *this week*, not someday.

### 5. Choose one improvement move (15 min)

Not ten moves. One. Examples sized honestly: raise one price by one reasonable increment; kill one time-sink; automate one thing with a rule or template; write one thing due to a client; set up one spreadsheet that answers a question you keep re-asking. Write it down with a due date inside the next quarter. One per quarter is twelve real improvements a year, which is more than most change efforts ever produce.

### 6. Set the next quarter's small goal (10 min)

One number-level goal: a revenue figure, an expense ratio, a pot target, a project milestone. It goes at the top of next quarter's money-date checklist, so the future-you who sits down in ninety days starts by seeing whether past-you kept the promise. (Careful, past-you has been *through* a quarter.)

### 7. Say something kind, and leave (5 min)

Genuinely: name one thing that went right. An invoice that got paid, a launch that shipped, a tax pot at target, a week where the ten-minute ritual survived a bad month. The money date ends with the business feeling like a friend, not an audit — that's the whole trick to actually *wanting* to do it four times a year.

Close by confirming next date is on the calendar with the coffee pill and everything.

## The scoreboard I actually glance at

Five numbers, from the ledger, on the checklist, every quarter:

| Number | What it tells me |
|---|---|
| Quarter income | Is the machine alive? Any big swings to understand? |
| Quarter expenses | Creeping up? One category dragging a sled? |
| Net profit | The number the tax pot drinks from |
| Tax pot balance | Never less than the [target %](taxes.md) × profit-to-date |
| Subscriptions count (and $) | Upgrades in, junk out; aim: smaller every quarter |

## The gentle version (ADHD-friendly, officially part of the manual)

Bad-brain days don't get to veto the whole date. The fallback is **three 10-minute sprints, at any hour, possibly on three different days**:

- **Sprint 1 — the books.** Sweep `Uncategorized`, totals, fill in the three scoreboard numbers. Stop. Walk away if you walked away.
- **Sprint 2 — the pots.** Move the tax percentage. Judge two subscriptions max, then close.
- **Sprint 3 — the plan.** One improvement move, one goal, calendar the next date, say the kind thing.

That's the entire meeting spread across a week if needed. Both versions count. The gentle version is what kept *my* streak alive through a deployment month, a conference, and one spectacular bout of post-launch brain fog.

Two more ADHD-friendly moves that earn their place:

- **Body-double it.** The date at a friend's kitchen table or a quiet library table turns a solo chore into a co-working hangout.
- **Make the checklist physical.** Print [the money-date checklist](quarterly-money-date.md), fill by hand. A pen cannot open a browser tab.

## What does *not* happen at a money date

- **No big strategy pivots.** "Should I start a second product line?" is a decision-journal question ([the playbook has a whole template](../small-business-ai-playbook)), not a 9:47 a.m. Wednesday-money-date question just because the numbers were open.
- **No spreadsheet sculpting.** Pretty formatting is a procrastination costume. Numbers in cells, done.
- **No comparing myself to anyone on the internet with a revenue chart.** The scoreboard compares this quarter to last quarter. That comparison is the only one with access to the actual data.

## The year, seen from the money dates

- **Q1 date (late March):** new year's ledger fresh, prior-year [year-end checklist](year-end.md) already done, so this one starts clean.
- **Q2 date (late June):** halfway audit — pot pacing, insurance renewal season top-of-mind, get the [annual-report ritual](annual-report.md) done if it's a filing year.
- **Q3 date (late September):** the big estimated-tax check-in (the [January payment](taxes.md) needs real money ready) and next-year calendar.
- **Q4 date (mid-December):** gentle wind-down, run the [year-end checklist](year-end.md) *from* this one, set next year's first goal.

Four sittings. Under six hours a year of actual business judgment. And with that, the whole money side of the company becomes a *habit* instead of a weather system.
