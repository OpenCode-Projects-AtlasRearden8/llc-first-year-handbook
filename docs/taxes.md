# Estimated taxes

This is the chapter everyone braces for. Here's the pleasant surprise: for a small single-owner LLC, estimated taxes are **a savings habit with four due dates attached.** The savings habit is entirely yours (and it's the fun part). The due dates belong to two names — the IRS and the Indiana Department of Revenue — and this chapter makes both of them feel human-sized.

> The standing promise of this handbook: this chapter is **orientation only, never tax advice.** It teaches the mental model and the vocabulary that round numbers hang on. Whether these concepts apply to your exact situation — that's the [professional's](professionals.md) job, and asking exactly once is not expensive.

## Why the money even exists

When you were an employee, your employer withheld taxes from every paycheck before you saw a cent. Now you're the boss — and the boss (you) forgot to withhold from the employee (also you). The money didn't disappear; it's just still sitting in your account, wearing a little costume we call **profit**.

**Estimated taxes** (the IRS calls these *estimated tax payments*) are how business owners hand back their share during the year, in four chunks, instead of one giant gulp in April. The IRS charges a small underpayment penalty when too little goes in too late. The whole game is making the pockets match expectations.

## The three pricing levels of profit

Before round numbers, know *what kinds* of tax hide in that tax pot. Three levels, in plain sentences:

1. **Federal income tax.** Brackets, filing thresholds, dependent shenanigans — the standard America tax puzzle everyone already knows. It applies to LLC profit exactly like it applies to any other income of yours.
2. **Self-employment tax.** The one employees never saw: Social Security + Medicare. Workers *and* employers split it — an employee sees 7.65% on a stub; a self-employed person pays both halves, which lands at about **15.3%** on most profit (up to each year's Social Security cap, then a much smaller amount above it). Plain translation: profit costs proportionately more payroll-tax than a wage, and that's normal for all of us.
3. **Indiana tax, for launches like mine.** Indiana charges individuals a flat state income tax — **2.95% for tax year 2026** (scheduled to drop to 2.90% in 2027; the [Indiana DOR rates page](https://www.in.gov/dor/resources/tax-rates-and-reports/rates-fees-and-penalties) is the current source). *Plus* your **county income tax**, a second line that varies by county (they run a range; find yours on the DOR's Departmental Notice #1 chart). Indiana total state-side is usually somewhere between 3% and 6% for typical counties.

Add rule zero: **taxes flow through to you personally, they are not the LLC's.** By default a single-member LLC doesn't pay tax *as an entity* — it's "disregarded," and the profit goes on your own **1040, Schedule C**. This is the friendliest possible default for year one: no corporate return, no separate federal tax ID filing season, just you and the forms you already knew.

## The round-number pot

Here is the entire active ingredient of this chapter. Commonly used by self-employed people as a starting rule of thumb — **verify your own number with a pro or an actual calculator each year**:

> **Keep 25%–30% of net profit for taxes.** Lower commonsense edge for small side-income on top of a W-2 job; higher edge when profit grows, or when it's your main income, or if you live in a heftier state-tax place.

Worked example with numbers that are easy to double-check in one's head: suppose the LLC clears **$10,000 of net profit** for the year.

- 25% of $10,000 = $2,500
- 30% of $10,000 = $3,000
- That's about **$250–$300 per month**, or $625–$750 per quarter in the pot

Move it at every [money date](money-dates.md), watch the pot fill, and the IRS never hosts a drama in your life. If a quarter's profit was unusually good, the *percentage* stays the same — the pot simply fills faster, exactly as intended.

Two honest adjustments people make to that flat rule (again: orientation, not gospel):

- **If you're also an employee somewhere** — like a nurse working shifts — check whether your W-2 withholding already covers much of your year's total tax. If it does, your LLC's pot can run much lighter. This is the single most common reason not to blanket-apply the 25% figure; a pro or the IRS worksheet (next section) can right-size it.
- **When profit gets big** (into numbers where brackets, credits and phase-outs start moving), round numbers get replaced by a professional number. You'll know when, and [here are the triggers](professionals.md).

## The safe harbor, in ladder language

The IRS doesn't actually demand you predict your own income perfectly. It offers two ladders, and climbing *either one* protects you from underpayment penalties (mostly, in ordinary circumstances):

- **Ladder A: small safe harbor.** Pay at least **90% of this year's** actual tax as we go, via estimates or withholding.
- **Ladder B: last year's ladder.** Pay **100% of last year's total tax** during this year (110% if last year's AGI was over roughly $150,000) via estimates or withholding — *regardless of what this year turns out to be*.

Ladder B is the friend-made-of-concrete: look at last year, meet its number, and the year is covered even if this year exploded upward. If you had *no* tax liability last year at all (say, the year before the LLC launched), the special first-year rules mean estimates often aren't required until income actually starts — read the [IRS estimated-taxes page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/estimated-taxes) for your situation, or bring it to a pro.

And the honest sneaky shortcut: **if you still have a W-2 job, you can adjust W-4 withholding** at the day job to cover extra tax — estimates aren't always needed, that conversion is exact and free, and your payroll can handle it before the next cycle.

## The four due dates

The same calendar everyone uses has four estimate dates (they slide a little when each lands on a weekend or holiday — the IRS publishes any shifts):

| Payment period | Usually due |
|---|---|
| January–March | **April 15** |
| April–May | **June 15** |
| June–August | **September 15** |
| September–December | **January 15** (of the next year) |

Yes, the stretches are unequal (that June one covers only two months, the January one covers four). The quarters are lopsided for everyone; learn them once and stop questioning. Paying each date isn't hard:

- **Federal:** [IRS Direct Pay](https://www.irs.gov/payments/direct-pay) — no account, free, a few minutes; choose the right tax-year and "estimated tax". Mark it in the ledger in the `Tax Pot` space so it stays visible.
- **Indiana:** pay state estimates through the [Indiana DOR's INtime online system](https://intime.dor.in.gov/) — the same four dates generally apply to state estimates; the DOR pages carry the specifics.
- **The pot pays everything.** By now the money is sitting in pot 2, which is precisely why the system is calm. If the pot's short: pay the portion you have, and the [money date](money-dates.md) recalibrates the percentage next quarter. The recovery mode is built in, not a crisis.

## When estimates aren't needed (yet)

Year-one reality, stated warmly: **if the LLC had no profit, or the business was launched so late it made almost nothing, there may be nothing to estimate.** People panic about owing tax on money they haven't made. You don't. Estimates exist for *actual* profit; the moment profit shows up, the pot and the dates turn on. If profit arrives mid-year and you're nervous about first-year requirements — one [professional call](professionals.md) covers it for years.
